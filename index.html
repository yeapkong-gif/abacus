1. Meet the Abacus

Before any lesson, show your child the abacus above and let them touch it. Point out these three parts:

  • Frame — the wooden border that holds everything together.
  • Rods — the straight tracks the beads slide along. Each rod stands for a place: ones, tens, hundreds.
  • Beads — the round pieces that slide up and down. Each bead you push up toward the bar is worth 1.
  • The bar (counting line) — the invisible line near the top. A bead only "counts" once it has been pushed up to touch it.
Golden Rule: Beads pushed up = counted. Beads left down = not counted yet. That's the entire idea — everything else is just this rule repeated.

2. Counting 0–9 on One Rod

Use just one rod to begin. This matches how a 5-year-old already counts on fingers, so it feels familiar.

  1. Point to the rod. Say: "All the beads are resting down. That means zero."
  2. Push one bead up. Ask: "How many beads are touching the line now?"
  3. Push up one more. Count together out loud: "1, 2."
  4. Keep going one bead at a time up to 9, counting aloud together each time.
  5. Push all the way back down and say "zero" — this teaches that beads can reset.
Say this out loud: "Every bead you push up adds one more. Let's count them together as we push."
Practice for 5 years old: Call out a number from 0–9 and ask your child to push up exactly that many beads without counting one-by-one first — just from memory of what the number "looks like" on the rod.

3. Place Value: Ones, Tens, Hundreds

Once counting to 9 on a single rod is easy, add a second rod to its left. This is the biggest "aha" moment of the whole abacus — each rod is worth ten times the rod to its right.

  1. Label the rightmost rod Ones, the next one Tens, and the next one Hundreds (sticky notes work well under the real abacus).
  2. Explain: "A bead on the Ones rod is worth 1. The exact same-looking bead on the Tens rod is secretly worth 10."
  3. Build the number 24: push up 2 beads on the Tens rod, and 4 beads on the Ones rod.
  4. Read it together left to right: "2 tens and 4 ones... twenty-four."
Say this out loud: "The rod doesn't change — only its place changes what each bead is worth."
Practice for 7 years old: Call out numbers like 15, 40, 103 and have your child build them bead-by-bead, then say the number back to you. Also try it backward: build a number silently, and ask them to read it off the beads.

4. Simple Addition (No Regrouping)

Both kids — start simple, then add tens for the 7-year-old

Example: 3 + 4

  1. Push up 3 beads on the Ones rod. Say: "This is our starting number, 3."
  2. Now push up 4 more beads on the same rod, counting "4, 5, 6, 7" as you go (continuing from 3).
  3. Read the total beads touching the bar: 7. So 3 + 4 = 7.
Key idea: Addition on the abacus is simply "push up more beads and keep counting." There is nothing more complicated happening — this is why it builds real number sense, not memorized tricks.
Age 7 — the "10 Rule" (regrouping / carrying)

Example: 7 + 5

Here the Ones rod runs out of room — there are only 9 beads. This is where the second rod earns its job.

  1. Push up 7 beads on the Ones rod.
  2. Try to push 5 more — but only 2 beads are left! Push those 2 up (now the rod shows 9).
  3. You still owe 3 more beads, but the rod is full. Here's the trick: push all 9 Ones beads back down to zero, and instead push up 1 bead on the Tens rod. Ten ones became one ten.
  4. Now push up the 3 beads you still owed, onto the now-empty Ones rod.
  5. Read the result: 1 ten and 2 ones = 12.
Say this out loud: "Ten little beads on one rod always trade for one big bead on the rod next door."
Practice for 7 years old: Try 8+6, 9+9, and 6+7. Every time the Ones rod would need to pass 9, stop and do the "trade": clear the Ones rod, add 1 to Tens, then finish counting the remainder onto Ones.

5. Simple Subtraction

Both kids

Example: 8 − 3

  1. Push up 8 beads on the Ones rod. Say: "This is what we're starting with."
  2. Push down 3 of those beads, counting "8 down to 7, 6, 5" as each one drops.
  3. Count what's still touching the bar: 5. So 8 − 3 = 5.
Age 7 — borrowing

Example: 12 − 5

  1. Build 12: 1 bead on Tens, 2 beads on Ones.
  2. You need to take away 5, but the Ones rod only has 2 beads on it — not enough!
  3. Trade the other way: push the 1 Tens bead back down, and instead push up all 10 beads on the Ones rod (10 ones = the ten you borrowed). Ones rod now shows 12... wait, that's too many — so really: clear the Tens bead, and add 10 beads worth to Ones, giving Ones a total of 12 beads' worth of counting room to work with.
  4. Now take away 5 from that: count down from 12 to 7.
  5. Result: 7.
Simplify for a first lesson: Borrowing is the hardest abacus skill — many 7-year-olds aren't ready until they're very confident with addition regrouping first. Feel free to skip borrowing for a few weeks and revisit it later.

7. Practice Games

Race to 20 Age 5

Take turns pushing up 1 or 2 beads at a time on a single rod (using two rods once you pass 9). Whoever pushes the bead that reaches exactly 20 wins.

Guess My Number Age 7

Build a number on the Tens and Ones rods while your child isn't looking. They open their eyes and must read the number from the beads alone.

Bead Flash Both

Push up a number of beads for one second, then cover the abacus. Ask "how many did you see?" Great for building instant number recognition.

Ten Trade Hunt Age 7

Call out addition problems that require a "10 trade" (7+6, 8+9, 6+8...). Every correct trade earns a sticker.

8. Tips for Teaching Two Different Ages Together

  • Keep sessions short: 10–15 minutes is plenty for a 5-year-old; 15–20 minutes for a 7-year-old. Stop while it's still fun.
  • Teach the 5-year-old first, let the 7-year-old "assist": Older kids love being the helper — this reinforces their own skills while you teach the younger one.
  • Always say the number out loud while moving beads. The verbal count links the physical action to the abstract number.
  • Common mistake to watch for: kids pushing beads up without counting them — always pause and ask "how many is that?" instead of assuming they're tracking it.
  • Don't rush to addition. A child who can instantly recognize any number 0–9 on the beads (without recounting from zero) is ready for addition. Not before.
  • Praise the process, not just correct answers — "I like how you counted out loud" matters more than a right result at this stage.
